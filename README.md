# REFR/ACT Playbook

**English** · [中文](README.zh.md)

> Before you build, let someone's actual life change your idea.

## We want to start one question earlier

“What could we make?” is a good question. It gets people to open an editor, find a whiteboard, and start talking.

But we keep coming back to the question before it: **whose problem is this?** Does that person need another app? Would our solution still work without reliable internet, with only one hand available, or without time to learn the interface we are so proud of?

This is what leaves us dissatisfied with some hackathons. The distance from an idea to a demo keeps getting shorter. The time spent understanding the problem does not necessarily grow with it. Two days later, the features work and the pitch sounds convincing, but the person the project was meant to help may never have entered the conversation.

REFR/ACT wants to change that order. Understand the situation. Let other people's perspectives, needs, and constraints change the approach. Then build. We do not mean waiting until every question is answered. We mean knowing what we are testing, and being willing to change direction when the evidence says we should.

That is **Refract**. An idea does not have to keep traveling in its original direction after it encounters the real world.

## We are high school students. The things we make can still matter.

At PRISMS, a STEM school, research teaches us to ask questions, look for evidence, and check our conclusions. We want another opportunity alongside that: to turn our own ideas into something another person can try.

Research and making do not need to compete. Careful questions make a project less dependent on assumptions; building exposes problems that a written plan can miss.

You should not need an impressive project before you arrive. You might code, draw, take things apart, ask good questions, or simply care about something inconvenient in your school day. Any of those can be a starting point.

We hope you leave with more than a presentation file. We want you to have a specific experience to remember: **someone took my idea seriously, and I was able to move it forward.**

## What a hackathon actually is

A hackathon is a time-limited, collaborative project event. Teams identify a problem, explore approaches, build a prototype, test and revise it, then demonstrate their work and answer questions. A prototype might be an app, a piece of hardware, a data tool, or a model that lets someone try the central idea. Nobody needs to turn it into a finished product in a day.

Coding helps, but it is not the only useful contribution. Understanding needs, designing interactions, testing results, and explaining trade-offs are real work. Mentors can help you get unstuck; they should not decide what your project becomes.

Each team chooses a primary track, with separate awards for the three tracks. All projects should practice all three habits:

- **Reflect:** Understand whose need you are addressing and which parts are still assumptions.
- **Refract:** Let feedback, constraints, and alternative approaches change your judgment. AI suggestions can be rejected too.
- **Act:** Make a version that can be tested. Explain where it helps and where it does not.

## Our next step is not just a bigger headcount

We would love more people to take part. Before that, we want to know whether a first-time participant found something meaningful to do, whether feedback changed a team's direction, and whether anyone still wants to open their project a month later.

A bigger group photo cannot answer those questions.

Our order is a first event we can honestly review, then a small gathering people want to return to, then an organization the next students can run. Cross-school collaboration and longer project support are possibilities, not promises to announce before we have the people, time, and demand. [The roadmap explains the steps.](docs/en/roadmap.md)
