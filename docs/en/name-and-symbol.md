# Why Refract? Why these lines?

[Home](../../README.md) · [中文](../zh/name-and-symbol.md)

## A name, and an order of working

To refract is to change direction at a boundary between media. What interests us is not simply that light looks beautiful. It is that an encounter can change where something goes.

Someone arrives with “I want to make this.” They meet a potential user and a teammate, then encounter limits on time, money, equipment, and their own understanding. If they leave able only to repeat their original sentence, something about that encounter has been wasted.

An idea should pass through those conversations before becoming action. That does not mean averaging everybody's opinions. It means noticing what changed our judgment and what still needs testing.

We write **REFR/ACT** so that ACT stands out. The slash can be read as a short pause: look first, then make something. It is a pause, not a place to stay forever.

**Refer to the human. Act with context.**

The human is not an abstract “user segment.” It is someone with habits, limited time, and perhaps no interest at all in using our project.

## Reading the mark

The existing symbol has uneven, pointed lines: a long horizontal line approaches from the left, a diagonal passes through the meeting point, and lines open out to the right. Since this is a text-only book, here is how we propose reading it.

**The incoming line is the first idea.** You may already know the model, the feature list, or even the name. It is clear, but it may point in only one direction. That is fine; everyone starts somewhere.

**The meeting point is where the idea encounters people.** Someone finds the button difficult to use. Someone asks what happens offline. Someone says their actual problem is not the one you described. The useful moment is not necessarily when everyone says “cool.” It may be when the plan changes.

**The outgoing lines are the possibilities that become visible.** They need not be equally long or all turn into products. One approach continues, one is rejected, and another waits for a test. We want to retain evidence of choices, rather than retell the process as an uninterrupted sequence of correct decisions.

The diagonal can be read as a boundary where an idea meets constraints and gains another direction. This echoes refraction; it is not an optical diagram. There is no need to assign every line a literal angle, track, or physical quantity.

## The history behind the prism image

Newton recorded work on refraction in 1666 and published his paper on light and colours in 1672. His experiments supported the view that white light contains differently coloured components: a prism separates them, and recombination produces white light. The glass was not simply creating colour. The [Newton Project's introduction to his scientific writings](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/texts/introduction) documents this background.

Our metaphor is about noticing differences inside something we initially treated as one thing. “Students need a learning tool” can hide very different needs: organizing notes, understanding a question, or finding uninterrupted time. A common label does not make those needs identical.

That is our interpretation, not a physics-based proof of an organizing philosophy. A prism cannot tell us whom to serve. We still have to ask people.

## Does a resemblance to a star map establish an origin?

No. Radial diagrams also appear in astronomy. The Voyager Golden Record cover, for example, locates the solar system relative to 14 pulsars; [NASA explains the diagram](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/).

That history is documented, but a similar outline does not establish a design lineage. We are not calling our mark a redraw of that map or borrowing another organization's origin story. Refraction is the more direct reading for our name and purpose.

## What we want the mark to remind us to ask

- Whose perspective informed this decision?
- Which piece of feedback actually changed something?
- Have we confused our current direction with the only possible answer?

If we keep asking, the mark does more than identify an event.

*Design note: the team has not identified a specific historical reference for the original mark. This interpretation was developed in October 2026 from the existing shape, name, and event purpose. The linked histories are context, not a retrospective claim about the designer's process.*
