## 1. Decide what you are building

A hackathon is a time-limited, collaborative project event. Students form teams, identify a problem, explore possible solutions, and build a prototype: a first version others can try, such as an app, a hardware model, or a data tool. Teams test and revise their work, then demonstrate it and answer judges’ questions. Mentors and optional workshops support learning, but students own the project and its decisions. It is not simply a coding-speed contest or a requirement to launch a finished product. Research, design, engineering, communication, and teamwork all matter.

Before asking for sponsorship or opening registration, put these six answers on one page:

| Question | Current REFR/ACT plan | Still to confirm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For whom? | PRISMS students and invited nearby middle/high school students; 30–60 expected | Scope of outside participation, capacity, entry process |
| When? | April 17, 2027; one day | Campus availability and final schedule |
| Where? | PRISMS campus | Rooms, equipment, staffing, supervision |
| What will teams do? | Teams of 2–5 submit a prototype and a brief process account | Prompt boundaries and available resources |
| How will it be judged? | Separate awards for each track; consider both outcome and process | Judges, weights, tie-breaking |
| How will it be paid for? | Seek cash and in-kind support | School financial account, approvals, reimbursements |

**Pause point:** If the venue, rules for students from other schools, on-site supervision, or financial process is unclear, do not announce it as confirmed. Keep preparing the plan and prospective contacts, but do not promise an event the team cannot yet deliver.

## 2. Give the work owners

The danger for a small team is that “everyone can handle it” quietly becomes “no one did.” A core group of **5–7 student organizers** is a workable starting point. One person can cover two roles, but each task needs one final owner.

| Role | Required deliverables |
| --- | --- |
| Overall coordination | Master timeline, weekly meetings, decision log, risk list; consolidate questions for the school |
| School liaison | Space, faculty advisor, proctors, outside students, visitors, and parent/guardian permission processes |
| Program and participants | Registration, confirmation emails, track rules, mentor/judge communication, on-site questions |
| Technology | Wi-Fi, power, projection, submission system, AI/API access and load testing |
| Partnerships and finance | Sponsor outreach, benefit records, budget versions, school account and purchasing/reimbursement process |
| On-site operations | Room map, supplies, check-in, food, timekeeping, incident contacts |

A 20–30 minute weekly meeting is enough if it ends with three things: **who owns what, when it is due, and what is blocking it**. Keep consequential decisions in a shared record, not only in chat.

## 3. Work backward from event day

| When | What should exist by the end of this stage |
| --- | --- |
| T−12 to T−10 weeks | One-page event definition; school point of contact; preliminary feasibility of space and outside participation; draft budget |
| T−10 to T−8 weeks | House Game qualifier plan, date, eligibility, published selection rubric, and permission path; prospective sponsor and community partner list |
| T−8 to T−6 weeks | House Game qualifier completed; two PRISMS representative teams selected or formed under the published rubric; technical, judging, and process issues recorded; main-event rules revised |
| T−6 to T−4 weeks | Main registration published after approval; mentor/judge outreach confirmed; submission system usable |
| T−4 to T−2 weeks | Participant list, room layout, food count, supply orders, volunteer shifts |
| T−14 to T−7 days | Second technical test; safety and visitor process; event guide sent to all participants |
| T−7 to T−1 days | On-site walkthrough, printed backups, final staffing; rules version frozen |
| Event day | Run schedule, log incidents, collect submissions, judge, and award |
| T+1 to T+14 days | Thank-yous, project archive, financial close, anonymous feedback, retrospective, public recap |

Do not skip the House Game pilot to save time. It is a short internal qualifier, not a miniature version of the main hackathon; it also reveals what is most likely to fail at the main event.

## 4. Run the House Game qualifier and pilot

**Proposed format:** PRISMS House Game internal qualifier, date TBD before April 17. PRISMS students enter individually in a short, bounded challenge. A rubric published before entry would be used to select or form **two PRISMS representative teams** for the main REFR/ACT event. The challenge is also a pilot for the event format and technology, not a full hackathon. Confirm eligibility, selection and team-formation rules, permissions, and school supervision before announcing it.

Treat the qualifier as a rehearsal with observation notes:

- [ ] Use a short, bounded prompt to test the explanations of the **Reflect / Refract / Act** tracks and whether the published rubric can fairly assess individual entries.
- [ ] Observe where students use AI: framing the problem, researching, coding, designing, debugging, or changing direction.
- [ ] Ask each entrant for a short reflection: What did AI suggest? What was accepted, rejected, or changed? Which important judgment did the student make?
- [ ] Trial the published selection rubric and record how the two representative teams are selected or formed; identify ambiguities for future revisions without changing criteria retroactively.
- [ ] Measure the actual time needed for check-in, challenge briefing, reminders, submission, demos, and feedback.
- [ ] Test school computers, available free/approved AI tokens, and local-model options; record what works and what does not.
- [ ] From House Game use and/or a separate load test, assess whether available infrastructure can reliably support **approximately 24 concurrent AI sessions**. This is a target to test, not a confirmed capability.

**Hold a retrospective within 48 hours of the qualifier.** For each issue, record what happened, its implication for April 17, and who will fix it by when. Revise the rules, technical plan, and on-site workflow at least once before scaling up.

## 5. Confirm space, people, and permissions

Send the school one consolidated, answerable list rather than scattered requests across many emails.

| Confirm with the school | Why ask now? |
| --- | --- |
| April 17 space and capacity | Main work area, opening/closing and demo area, mentor/workshop space, rest/food area; lab or engineering space if hardware is included |
| Faculty advisor, school event contact, proctors | Student organizers cannot replace school supervision; PRISMS determines proctor numbers and duties |
| Judges | Plan for about 3–4; confirm school participation, outside-judge entry, and judging window |
| Financial account and responsible adult | Receive sponsorship, record funds, authorize purchasing/reimbursement and awards; do not collect event money in a student's personal account |
| Students from other schools | Invitation scope, visitor check-in, parent/guardian consent, transportation, campus rules, emergency contacts |
| IT and equipment | Wi-Fi, power, displays, school computers/AI access, advance tests; plan for about 1–2 technical support people |
| Other on-site staff | About 3–5 mentors and 2–3 check-in/logistics helpers; adjust to school requirements and approved attendance |
| Food and safety | Catering, allergies, cleanup, access control, first aid, emergency contact |

**Deliverables:** A school-confirmed room map, a contacts-and-responsibilities sheet, and an approved version of what may be announced publicly. Until these exist, use “proposed” and “pending confirmation” rather than implying a school commitment.

## 6. Build the budget and partnerships

List costs before asking for sponsors. Otherwise, it is easy to acquire a collection of credits while still lacking essential supplies or usable funds.

The budget needs at least five categories: **awards, food, hardware/consumables, publicity and on-site supplies, and contingency**. For each item, record quantity, estimate, payment method, approver, and whether it is essential or reducible. Quotes and purchases follow school procedures.

REFR/ACT's current fundraising goal is **$10,000+ in combined cash and in-kind support**, including a potential **$3,000+ student prize pool**. These are goals, not money already secured. Final sponsorship and awards require school approval.

Classify partners by the resources they can offer, not only by company size:

| Category | Useful support | Confirm in outreach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cloud computing / AI tools | Credits, API tokens, technical staff, or a workshop—for example, from AWS | Eligibility for minors/schools, expiration, concurrency limits, data rules |
| Incubators / startup communities | Mentors, judges, project feedback, introductions—for example, through YC-related founder networks | Who will actually attend, availability, conflicts of interest |
| Developer / local communities | Outreach, volunteers, mentors, workshops, or space | Reach, point of contact, campus-entry and minor-participant rules |
| Hardware / developer tools | Boards, sensors, licenses, loaner equipment, prizes | Shipping, damage responsibility, compatibility, on-site support |
| Cash sponsors | Awards, consumables, food, essential costs | Payment route, school approval, appropriate recognition |

**Minimum outreach log:** Organization, contact, specific request, possible contribution, restrictions, next step, and owner. An email sent is not a sponsorship secured; count support as usable only after written confirmation and school approval. Keep benefits honest: recognition, product trials, technical talks, and project feedback are discussable; student recruiting and automatic ownership of student work are not part of the plan.

## 7. Registration and participant communication

A first-time participant should understand the registration page: what the event is, who may join, date and place, team size, three tracks, what to bring, whether food is provided, how permissions work, and when confirmation will arrive.

Collect only information needed to run the event. Store and use minors' personal information under school rules. Public participant lists, photos, and project displays require the relevant consent and school compliance.

Send at least three messages:

1. **Registration receipt:** What was received, when a decision will be sent, and how to reach the team.
2. **Pre-event guide (about a week ahead):** Check-in time, map, team/track choice, equipment, software, parent/visitor requirements, and food information.
3. **Day-before reminder:** Final time and place, what to do if late or unable to attend, and the on-site contact.

Maintain a waitlist. If someone withdraws, fill the place in the established order; do not change the rule ad hoc in private messages.

## 8. Technology and supplies

Test the path **a student will actually take**, not just an organizer's administrator account. On an ordinary student device, start with Wi-Fi and complete login, AI/API access, and project submission end to end.

- [ ] Wi-Fi coverage and concurrency in the main rooms; if there is a guest network, confirm outside students can use it.
- [ ] Outlets, extension cords, cable routing, charging safety; keep walkways clear.
- [ ] Projector, adapters, audio, timer display, and a backup demo method.
- [ ] Submission form/repository link, deadline, file format, and receipt after upload.
- [ ] AI/API account distribution, spending cap, rate limits, and a fallback when tokens run out.
- [ ] Feasibility of local models or school infrastructure and results of the roughly 24-session load test.
- [ ] Hardware kits, tool check-out/return, consumables, damage, and inventory.
- [ ] Printed check-in sheet, offline schedule, judge score sheets, and backup emergency contacts.

For every test, record date, device/account, steps, result, failure, and owner of the fix. “The account exists” does not mean a participant can use it on event day.

## 9. Tracks, submissions, and judging

**Reflect, Refract, and Act are three separately entered and awarded tracks—not three mandatory stages every team must complete in sequence.** Their names also suggest a shared practice: understand the problem, examine it from another angle, then build and test something. Each team chooses the track that best fits its project's emphasis. If a project spans tracks, settle its primary track before the registration deadline; do not reclassify it after judging to fit an award.

| Track | Central question | Evidence judges should see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reflect** | Did you identify a problem worth solving? | Target users, real needs, tested assumptions, and how the prototype responds |
| **Refract** | Did another perspective or an AI tool genuinely change your judgment? | At least one compared alternative; advice accepted, rejected, or modified; and why |
| **Act** | Can the idea work and withstand a test? | Working/demonstrable prototype, test or iteration record, technical decisions and limits |

Every track should consider both **outcome** (usefulness, technical execution, creativity, prototype quality) and **process** (understanding the problem, reasoning, multiple perspectives, judgment in AI use). Reflect should not become a research-report-only track, nor Act a contest of polish alone. Every team must make something demonstrable.

**Common submission package:** Project name, team members, selected track, one-sentence problem statement, demo link or live demonstration, major decisions, and a brief **AI Process Reflection**: Where did AI participate? What outputs were rejected or rewritten? Which decisions did the team make itself? Teams that did not use AI can say why; no one needs to use it just to fill in a field.

**Finalize before judging:** Rubric and weights, number of awards per track, judge recusal, ties, late submissions, whether pre-event code or project foundations are allowed, and cross-team collaboration. Give judges the same rules and a brief calibration example before scoring. Publish the awards budget and distribution process once the school confirms them.

## 10. Run the day

This is a **sample one-day sequence**, not a final schedule. Assign actual clock times only after school hours, rooms, and supervision are confirmed.

| Sequence | What the on-site lead does | Done when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before doors open | Test Wi-Fi, projection, submission; set up check-in, signs, water, and power areas | An ordinary student device completes the full path |
| Check-in | Verify roster and permissions; distribute schedule, rules, and help channel | Every team knows its place and contact |
| Opening | Explain tracks, deliverables, deadlines, conduct and safety rules | Every team has the rules link |
| Team/project confirmation | Help students without teams; confirm teams, tracks, and problems | Team list exists and sizes comply |
| Build | Mentors circulate; organizers monitor time, technology, food, and room conditions | Issues have an owner and a record |
| Submission reminders | Give two advance reminders of format and deadline | Teams can see their submission receipts |
| Demos and judging | Use a fixed order and equal presentation time; collect scores | All eligible teams have equal opportunity to present |
| Awards and close | Announce results by track; explain feedback and departure | Awards recorded, equipment returned, headcount checked |

Use one shared run sheet: **time, action, owner, backup, status**. Announce temporary rule changes, such as a deadline extension, to every team at once; record who made the decision and when.

## 11. When something goes wrong

Protect people and fairness before protecting the show. Assign one on-site lead to communicate decisions so three organizers do not give three different answers.

| Situation | First response | Follow-up decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wi-Fi / AI service outage | Log start time and affected teams; switch to local/offline or backup tools | One lead decides and announces any uniform extension |
| Submission system fails | Open a backup form or email/offline intake; retain timestamps | Apply the same late-submission rule to all teams |
| Mentor/judge absent | Use the prearranged substitute list | Recheck judging time and conflicts |
| Team size changes | Check rules and permissions; do not grant an improvised verbal exception | Resolve consistently before the deadline and document it |
| Safety, health, or student departure issue | Contact the designated school proctor / emergency contact immediately | Follow school procedure; event pace yields to student safety |
| Budget or sponsor changes | Freeze unapproved purchases and promises | Reprioritize using essential/reducible budget labels |

## 12. Close, thank, and learn

The work is not over when the event ends. While memories are fresh, aim to finish these steps within two weeks:

- [ ] Reconcile equipment returns, cleanup, receipts, sponsor commitments, and awards.
- [ ] Send specific thanks to participants, mentors, judges, school contacts, sponsors, and community partners.
- [ ] Publish projects and photos only with consent; keep unauthorized personal information out of the public repository.
- [ ] Ask briefly: What helped most? Where did you get stuck? Would you come again?
- [ ] Review registration-to-attendance, technical failures, submission rate, judging time by track, and budget variance.
- [ ] Write each improvement as “what to change next time + who owns it,” not “communication should improve.”

**A successful event is not measured only by dazzling demos.** Ask whether students can explain whom they built for, why they chose their approach, and which suggestions they set aside. Ask whether first-time participants had enough support, and whether the team handled disruptions fairly.

## To the next organizers

If you inherit this checklist one day, do not begin by worrying whether you can make a “perfect” event. Find people willing to work beside you. Find a school contact you can trust. Keep the first test small. Say what you do not yet know, then solve it one item at a time.

We hope a student walks in with a half-formed question and leaves knowing, “I can do this, too.” We hope the people who move tables, trace cables, revise rules, and write late-night emails have a moment to see that their care gave someone else the courage to try.

If this playbook helps the next team avoid a few wrong turns—and helps more ideas be heard, tested, and made real—then every small preparation mattered. **May we remember that what makes REFR/ACT shine is not the light on the stage, but the people standing both in it and beyond it.**
