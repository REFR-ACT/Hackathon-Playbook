# What we want to do after the event

[Home](../../README.md) · [中文](../zh/roadmap.md)

## Make “later” a little more specific

We could quickly write an ambitious plan: more schools, more participants, a larger venue. None of those phrases tells us what to do next week.

This roadmap starts closer to home. It describes steps we want to work toward, not a calendar backed by resources we already have. An item without an owner is not underway. An arrangement needing school approval stays marked pending until that approval exists.

One thing we would like to preserve is simple: after the event, a participant should still know whom to talk to about their project. Not necessarily about investment or a startup. Perhaps just about fixing the part that never worked, or finding one more person to try it.

## First: prepare the first event

**Between now and the main event, prioritize small things we can test.**

Confirm a school contact, date and venue feasibility, outside participation, supervision, and the financial route. Prepare the House Game individual challenge, the public rubric, and the process for selecting or forming two representative teams.

Then run it. Have students enter the tools, interpret the prompt, and submit work. Have judges apply the same rules. Record where people get stuck, how long steps take, and which explanations are unclear. “It went well” is not enough information.

The useful outputs are revised rules, a technical test record, and a task list with owners. Approximately 24 concurrent AI sessions remains a test target, not a capability to advertise before checking it.

**Condition for proceeding:** key approvals are resolved, qualifier problems are assigned and addressed, and registration materials can accurately describe the available resources. Otherwise, reduce the scope or adjust the arrangements.

## Second: make April 17 a day worth returning to

The main event is proposed for **April 17, 2027**, with **30–60 participants in teams of 2–5**, subject to school confirmation. The first edition does not need to be a community, incubator, technology exhibition, and recruitment event all at once.

We want three things first:

1. A newcomer can find a meaningful contribution, rather than only watching others.
2. Each team can explain a decision that changed because of feedback or testing.
3. Each team receives a specific suggestion it could act on afterward.

Participants should not have to promise to continue their projects to make our summary look good. Discovering that an approach is not worth pursuing can also be a useful outcome.

**What this stage leaves:** project descriptions shared with permission, a record of operational problems, anonymous feedback, and rules to improve. Those are more useful than declaring the day a complete success.

## Third: give the work somewhere to go afterward

**Within two weeks, aim to complete the practical closeout and review.** Return equipment, reconcile finances, send thanks, and address outstanding issues. Then write a review that includes what failed. Teams choose whether their projects are public; a showcase is not permission to publish everyone's code.

**Around a month afterward, try an optional project check-in.** This is a proposal: assign an owner and ask whether participants want it before scheduling it. A short session is enough. People can share what changed, where they are stuck, and what they want to try next.

If exams make a gathering unhelpful, offer an optional written update or cancel it. Continuing support should not become extra homework for everyone.

**Condition for proceeding:** participants want to continue, someone can organize it, and it does not depend on the same one or two people working indefinitely beyond their capacity.

## Fourth: try small build sessions before promising a permanent community

If follow-ups reveal demand, a subsequent semester could include occasional sessions where people bring unfinished work and help each other make progress on a specific problem.

We are not promising weekly access. Confirm space, supervision, equipment, and time before deciding the frequency. We would also like design, writing, hardware, and data contributors to feel that helping is not synonymous with coding.

With sufficient resources, possibilities include project matching, mentor office hours, small materials support, or equipment lending. **These are exploratory ideas, not services already available.** Budgets and owners need to exist before we publish an offer.

Cross-school exchanges can start small too: a few teams testing each other's projects or sharing an honest review. We do not need to announce a regional network at the beginning.

## Fifth: make it possible to continue after we graduate

An organization should not exist only in one person's message history.

After the first event, we want younger students who are interested in taking responsibility to join the review. Seeing how decisions are made is more useful than receiving a crowded folder just before handover.

Leave behind:

- Public rules, timelines, reusable templates, and anonymized reviews.
- Contacts, approval records, and financial materials in appropriate private channels.
- Reasons for major decisions and alternatives that were considered.
- Account-access and equipment handover arrangements, without passwords or secrets in the repository.

The next edition need not look identical. It can be smaller, use a different prompt, or revise the tracks. If it still starts with people and takes evidence seriously, changing the format does not mean losing Refract.

## How we would recognize progress

Rather than inventing an annual growth target, we want a few useful signals:

- Can newcomers tell how to begin and whom to ask for help? Ask through anonymous feedback.
- Can teams describe a decision changed by feedback or a test? Read the process reflections.
- Which teams voluntarily want to continue, and what do they need? Ask later; silence is not failure.
- Are finances reconciled and technical problems addressed? Have owners verify it.
- Are younger students willing to own a real task, rather than only appear on a list?

Report actual counts and response coverage. Two positive comments do not represent every participant. The plan can change: if build sessions turn out not to be useful, explain what happened and try a better form of support.

## One thing you can do now

You need not wait for the whole community plan. Use an [Issue](https://github.com/REFR-ACT/Hackathon-Playbook/issues) to describe a problem you care about, what you want to try, and one small part you could take responsibility for.

The future we want starts with somebody taking that part.

*Updated October 2026. These are plans and exploratory directions, not commitments of places, funding, or continuing support.*
